Why there is a third row

Tab shows you where to put your fingers but not what you are playing. Notation shows what you are playing but not where. I wanted both, so the fret numbers and the note names go on the page together.

You never type the names. Put a 4 on the D string and Fis turns up underneath it. Move the note to another string and the name changes with it. Dutch names, or English if you would rather have F♯ and B♭.

There is a bit of everything in those two bars. Quarters and eighths, a dotted quarter, a tie, an eighth rest, two ghost notes, a triplet, a slide into the second bar and a hammer-on coming out of it, chord names, and a repeat instead of writing the bars out twice.

Chords first, notes later

Some songs are easier to take apart from the top down. You get the chord progression on one listen, then go back and work out what the bass is actually doing under it. The whole shape goes in as one line of typing.

  1. Type the progression

    [Verse] Am | C | G G7 | Dm

  2. Get the bars

    __CHART__

  3. Work out the notes

    __FILLED__

It will show you what is in the chord

While you are filling a bar in, the panel shows the chord you are under, which notes are in it, and everywhere they sit on the neck. Click one of the dots and that note goes in. Saves counting frets to find the third.

Majors, minors, sevenths, sixths, sus and diminished, with the root spelled either way. Slash chords keep their bass note, which you will care about.

One bar per line, or use |. Two chords in a bar splits it between them. [Name] starts a new section. When you type a fret over a rest it becomes a note, and the chord stays where you put it.

What it does

Write it

  • Pick a note length, type fret numbers, space to move on.
  • Bar lines look after themselves.
  • Rests, dots, ties, triplets. Dead notes, hammer-ons, pull-offs, slides.
  • Staccato dots, and words like legato or let ring written under the staff.
  • Any time signature, and it can change from any bar — 7/8 and 5/4 as readily as 4/4.
  • Repeat marks with a count on them, and 1st and 2nd time bars.
  • Chord names above, lyrics below, and headings like Chorus.
  • Click a chord to see its notes on the neck, then click one to put it in.
  • Copy a phrase and paste it further on. Undo goes back as far as you like, and there is a redo for when you go too far.

Read it

  • Four, five or six strings, and alternate tunings. Change it and the notes move, not the music.
  • Octave numbers in Helmholtz, scientific, Yamaha or written pitch. Or none at all.
  • Or leave the note names out of it entirely, and read it as tab.
  • Set how many bars go on a line, and break lines and pages yourself.
  • Bar numbers, for when somebody says take it from 17.
  • A section that is just a repeat of an earlier one can say so in a word instead of being written out again.
  • Prints to PDF with none of the editor on it.
  • Full screen for a music stand: the editor gets out of the way and the music grows to fill the window.

Play it

  • Play from any note, or select a passage and hear only that.
  • It follows the repeats and the endings.
  • Loop four bars at 60% and bring them up to speed.
  • A click on every beat — in whatever metre the bar is in — and a bar of count-in before you come in.
  • Plain, P-style or J-style. Where a pickup sits along the string is what changes the tone, and that is what it models.
  • The page turns a line early, so you are never reading off the bottom of the screen.

How it's put together

One fileDownload it once and it keeps working.
Works offlineNo account, no sign-in, no connection.
Nothing leaves your laptopNo tracking, no uploads, no cookie banner.
Plain filesTranscriptions are plain .json. Keep them wherever you like.

What you get when you download. One file called bass-notation.html, which lands wherever your browser puts downloads. Double-click it and it opens in your browser like any other page — except it is now yours, it needs no connection, and nobody can take it away. Put it somewhere you will find it again, next to your transcriptions.

Worth knowing. The editor remembers what you were last working on, but the copy on this page and the copy on your own machine each remember their own. Before you move from one to the other, save your transcription with the Save button — that gives you a .json file you can open in either.

It runs in any current browser. Saving over the same file, where you pick a name once and every save after that writes straight back to it, needs Chrome or Edge. Those are the ones that let a page hang on to a file. Firefox and Safari do everything else the same, but Save gives you a fresh copy each time, the way a download does.

I built this for my own transcriptions and then tidied it up in case anyone else wants it. It is free.
